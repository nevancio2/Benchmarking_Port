What IBM Did

IRS Business Systems Modernization (BSM)

The IRS Business Systems Modernization (BSM) program is one of the most frequently cited federal legacy modernization case studies in the United States. For decades, the IRS operated on aging systems with deeply integrated business processes that federal oversight bodies — including the Government Accountability Office (GAO) and the Defense Business Board — described as appearing "unsolvable" due to their complexity and interdependence.

IBM's approach was not to rebuild the IRS's entire end-to-end process. Instead, IBM demonstrated success in deconstructing complexity across multiple interconnected subsystems independently — modernizing each one while keeping the primary tax processing business process continuously operational. The goal was to move IRS employees onto unified, integrated data from fragmented case management systems without triggering cascading failures across dependent systems.

The GAO formally documented this as a model case study on the value of shared services and phased federal modernization. The Defense Business Board subsequently adopted it as a formal reference for government-wide systems transformation strategy.

"The lesson is incremental deconstruction — modernizing complicated subsystems one at a time while keeping the primary business process running."

— IBM NCDOT Strategy Document, 2025
The Data Silo Problem IBM Solved

The Problem

Multiple revenue streams and case management systems were trapped in separate legacy environments. Each subsystem had its own data model, lifecycle, and access rules with no integrated reporting layer across them.

IRS employees could not get a unified view of a taxpayer's case. Data had to be re-entered manually between systems, creating errors and delays.

Why It Appeared Unsolvable

The subsystems were not merely old — they were deeply woven together. A change in one triggered unpredictable effects in others. Any traditional "rip-and-replace" approach risked catastrophic failure of tax collection for hundreds of millions of Americans.

IBM's Solution

IBM applied an incremental deconstruction strategy: identify each subsystem's boundary, modernize it as a discrete unit, and connect it to the emerging integrated enterprise data layer — one at a time, with zero downtime to tax processing operations.

NCDOT Relevance — Why This Case Matters to You
NCDOT Direct Parallel

NCDOT's Systems Are the IRS Problem — Reapplied to Transportation

NCDOT operates multiple modal systems — ferries, highways, transit, and tolls — each trapped in its own legacy data silo with no integrated reporting or cross-agency visibility. This is structurally identical to the IRS challenge IBM already solved.

  • Ferry systems have GPS/arrival data that does not communicate with transit dispatch
  • Toll collection revenue data is isolated from transit ridership and highway traffic data
  • Regional operators (CATS, GoTriangle, Wave Transit) each run independent systems with no statewide data layer

What NCDOT Can Take from This Case

The IRS case delivers one foundational insight for NCDOT: you do not have to rebuild everything to modernize everything. IBM's incremental deconstruction methodology means NCDOT can start with a single modal silo — ferries, for example — modernize it as a discrete subsystem, and then extend the unified data layer to highways, transit, and tolls one at a time.

This eliminates the political, financial, and operational risk of a "big bang" technology overhaul. NCDOT continues operating 24/7 transportation infrastructure throughout — just as the IRS never stopped collecting taxes during its modernization.

  • Pick one NCDOT modal silo as the starting point (e.g., NC Turnpike tolls or NCDOT Ferry Division)
  • Define the subsystem boundary clearly — what data flows in, what flows out
  • Modernize that subsystem using IBM's deconstruction methodology
  • Connect it to the emerging NCDOT integrated data platform
  • Repeat for each additional mode — highways, transit, maintenance
