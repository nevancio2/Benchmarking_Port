Dimension 1 — Throughput & Scale

~9.0 Million TEUs — The Nation's #2 Container Port

The Port of New York and New Jersey handled approximately 9.0 million TEUs in calendar year 2024, making it the second-busiest container port in the United States — second only to the LA/Long Beach complex. Within this benchmark's six-port set, NY/NJ is the largest port by a factor of more than 1.5x relative to Savannah. PANYNJ confirmed in its February 2025 press release that the port maintained its status as the second-busiest U.S. port for loaded containers.

NY/NJ's volume is not merely a function of infrastructure investment — it is a function of geography: the port serves the single largest consumer market in the United States, providing structural demand regardless of operational efficiency. The port surged 18.5% in 2021 (post-COVID demand spike) and has sustained elevated volumes through 2024.

Score: 98/100

Highest throughput score in benchmark. NY/NJ's structural geographic advantage — serving the New York metro area and broader Northeast — is irreplaceable by any competing port.

Dimension 2 — Operational Efficiency (CPPI)

Multi-Terminal Fragmentation — The Root Cause of Efficiency Underperformance

NY/NJ operates across five major container terminals under different private operators: APM Terminals Bayonne, GCT Bayonne, GCT New York (Howland Hook), Port Newark Container Terminal (PNCT), and Red Hook. These five terminals operate with different terminal operating systems, different equipment configurations, and different physical configurations. On the World Bank CPPI, NY/NJ typically scores in the lower-middle range of U.S. East Coast ports.

PANYNJ's role as pure landlord — leasing terminals to private operators — means it has limited direct levers over terminal productivity. The Bayonne Bridge raising (completed 2019, raising air draft clearance to 215 feet) was transformative for vessel size capability. The 50-foot channel and former air draft restriction are infrastructure constraints that Virginia's 55-foot depth and ample air draft clearance do not share.

Score: 62/100

Multi-terminal fragmentation, pure landlord governance model limiting operational levers, 50-foot channel depth ceiling for ULCV access, and historically mid-range CPPI performance constrain this score despite the port's massive size. Scale alone does not produce operational efficiency.

The Bayonne Bridge raising (completed 2019) was one of the most consequential East Coast port capital investments of the last decade — unlocking neo-Panamax vessel calls that were previously physically blocked from reaching Newark Bay terminals. Combined with the 50-foot channel, NY/NJ can now receive post-Panamax and some neo-Panamax vessels, though not fully loaded ULCVs above approximately 15,000 TEU. With 9.0 million TEUs moved in 2024 across multiple terminals, NY/NJ operates at approximately 82–85% of its ~10–11M TEU stated capacity ceiling.

Score: 80/100

Large, functional infrastructure with the Bayonne Bridge (now resolved) and 50-foot channel providing reasonable but not leading depth capability. The fragmented multi-terminal configuration and 50-foot depth ceiling (vs. Virginia's 55-foot target) constrain the score below the infrastructure leaders.

Dimension 4 — Financial Performance & Revenue Model

$7.1B Total Agency Revenue — Unmatched Multi-Facility Diversification

PANYNJ's financial structure is fundamentally different from every other port in this benchmark. PANYNJ is a multi-facility bi-state agency with total gross operating revenues of approximately $7.0–7.1 billion (FY2024/2025 budget) across all facilities — airports, bridges and tunnels, port marine, and PATH rail. Port/marine revenues are a minority component, estimated at approximately $450–500 million annually, or roughly 6–7% of total agency revenues.

Revenue per TEU (port segment only): ~$50–56/TEU — the lowest in this benchmark. This reflects the structural reality that a landlord port captures a fraction of total cargo revenue vs. an operator port like Savannah ($127/TEU). PANYNJ's AA- Fitch credit rating (the highest in this benchmark) is driven by its diversified multi-facility revenue base — no container market downturn could threaten PANYNJ's financial position given aviation and toll revenues.

Score: 88/100

Highest financial score in benchmark, driven by unmatched revenue diversification (airports + bridges/tunnels + port + PATH), AA- credit rating, and multi-facility financial resilience. Score would be lower measured solely on port-specific metrics (low revenue per TEU, landlord model revenue dilution), but total entity strength is genuinely superior.

Dimension 5 — Connectivity & Hinterland Reach

Largest U.S. Consumer Market — Structural Geographic Advantage

NY/NJ's geographic hinterland — the New York metro area (20+ million people), New Jersey, Connecticut, upstate New York, and the broader Northeast — is the largest, wealthiest immediate consumer base of any port in this benchmark. No infrastructure investment can replicate this advantage. All major global carriers call at NY/NJ; the port serves Asia, Europe, Latin America, Mediterranean, and Middle East trades comprehensively — the broadest carrier coverage in the benchmark.

ExpressRail provides on-dock rail at Port Newark and Howland Hook, but rail intermodal modal share at NY/NJ is lower than Virginia and Savannah relative to total TEUs, reflecting the truck-dominant logistics model of the Northeast.

Score: 72/100

Structural geographic advantage of serving the U.S.'s largest consumer market provides an irreplaceable asset. Deductions reflect relatively limited on-dock rail investment vs. Virginia and Savannah, heavy truck dependency, and the fragmented multi-terminal layout creating routing complexity.

Dimension 6 — Governance & Strategic Positioning

Bi-State Compact — The Governance Complexity Price of Diversification

PANYNJ's governance is the most unique and most constraining for port-specific decision-making in this benchmark. As a bi-state compact authority created in 1921 by New York and New Jersey, PANYNJ is governed by commissioners appointed by two governors with competing political agendas. Capital decisions for the port compete against airport redevelopments, bridge/tunnel projects, PATH modernization, and other PANYNJ priorities at the board level.

The landlord model further limits operational control: PANYNJ sets the infrastructure framework (channel depth, Bayonne Bridge, ExpressRail) but cannot directly manage crane productivity, terminal operating hours, or vessel scheduling at APM Terminals, GCT, or PNCT. This governance complexity is the port equivalent of a federated-without-standardization governance problem — the same root cause identified in transit benchmarks where fragmentation limits operational outcomes regardless of infrastructure investment.

Score: 70/100

Bi-state governance and multi-facility agency context limit port-specific agility and capital prioritization. The landlord model further reduces direct operational influence. These governance constraints are structural — the price of PANYNJ's multi-facility diversification that simultaneously drives its superior credit rating.
