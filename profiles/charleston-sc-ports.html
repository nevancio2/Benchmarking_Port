Dimension 1 — Throughput & Scale

Steady Growth, But Low Velocity Against Peers

Charleston's annual container throughput reached 2,518,553 TEUs in FY2025 (July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025), up 0.8% from 2,497,143 TEUs in FY2024, which was itself up 0.6% from 2,482,080 TEUs in FY2023. Within this six-port benchmark, Charleston ranks fifth in TEU throughput — ahead only of Port Everglades and PortMiami, and materially behind Savannah (~5.9M TEUs), Virginia (~3.5M TEUs), and NY/NJ (~9.0M TEUs).

The throughput score of 72/100 reflects Charleston's established mid-size position and its low growth velocity. The port is growing, but 0.6–0.8% annual TEU growth in an environment where Savannah is growing 9% in a single month represents a competitive gap that will compound over time if unaddressed. The Hugh K. Leatherman Terminal (HLT) Phase 1 adds incremental capacity but has not yet generated volume acceleration.

Dimension 2 — Operational Efficiency (CPPI)

Competent Operations; Lacks Automation Differentiator

Charleston operates across three primary container facilities: Wando Welch Terminal (WWT) — the main container terminal with approximately 4,700 linear feet of berth and 14 STS cranes; Hugh K. Leatherman Terminal (HLT) Phase 1 — with approximately 1,400 linear feet and 4 super post-Panamax cranes; and North Charleston Terminal (NCT), which handles breakbulk and limited containers. The 52-foot channel depth, completed in 2021, is the most important efficiency-enabling infrastructure development at the port in the last decade, allowing neo-Panamax and large ULCV vessels to call.

On the World Bank CPPI framework, Charleston performs competitively for its volume tier. The deepened channel enables larger vessel calls, which improve moves-per-port-hour (MPPH) scores. However, Charleston does not have the automation advantage of Virginia (no ASCs deployed at scale) and does not have the absolute scale of Savannah. Truck turn time at Wando Welch Terminal has been a noted pain point during peak periods due to the terminal's layout designed in an earlier era of smaller vessel calls.

Score: 72/100

Above-average infrastructure from the 52-foot channel deepening. Lacks Virginia's automation differentiator and Savannah's scale-driven efficiency, but performs solidly within its volume tier.

Dimension 3 — Infrastructure & Physical Capacity

52-Foot Channel — A Genuine Competitive Advantage

Charleston's most significant infrastructure achievement is the 52-foot channel depth — second only to Virginia's 55-foot target on the East Coast and meaningfully deeper than NY/NJ (50 ft), Port Everglades (50 ft), PortMiami (50 ft), and Savannah (47 ft). This is Charleston's single most important competitive infrastructure differentiator as ultra-large container vessels (ULCVs) proliferate. A 52-foot channel allows fully loaded neo-Panamax vessels to call at any tide.

The Hugh K. Leatherman Terminal is the transformative capacity project. Phase 1 is operational with 1,400 linear feet of berth and 4 super post-Panamax STS cranes, adding approximately 700,000 TEUs of annual capacity. When fully built out, HLT is planned for ~2.4 million TEUs annually, which would push the port into the 4.0–4.5 million TEU capacity range. The combined crane fleet totals approximately 18 STS cranes (14 at WWT + 4 at HLT Phase 1) — smaller than Virginia's 29 and Savannah's 38.

Dimension 4 — Financial Performance & Revenue Model

Thin Margins, Heavy Debt, and State Subsidy Dependence

Total operating revenues were $426.5 million in FY2025, a 5.6% increase from $404.0 million in FY2024. Operating income in FY2025 was $26.7 million — a return to profitability after a loss of ($2.3 million) in FY2024. The FY2024 operating loss indicates that at $404 million in revenues, SC Ports was not covering its operating costs without non-operating support. The 6.3% FY2025 operating margin is thin for a capital-intensive infrastructure business.

Non-operating revenues in FY2025 totaled $36.8 million — including $22.1 million in state grants and operating appropriations and $16.5 million in investment income. Not at WWT — Gap vs. Peers

Inland Port Greer (Upstate SC, near I-85): Opened 2013, connected via Norfolk Southern rail. Serves Upstate South Carolina, western North Carolina, and portions of Tennessee and Georgia — enabling SC Ports to compete for BMW, Michelin, and other Upstate manufacturing container flows.

Inland Port Dillon (NE South Carolina, I-95 corridor): Opened 2018, connected via CSX rail. Extends Charleston's reach toward cargo flows in southeastern North Carolina and the Eastern Seaboard.

The absence of on-dock rail at Wando Welch Terminal is a notable gap — rail at WWT requires truck drays to move containers between the terminal and the nearest rail ramp, adding time and cost compared to true on-dock rail at Virginia's VIG/NIT and Savannah's Mason Mega Rail Terminal.

Score: 73/100

Two inland ports are genuine strategic assets. The absence of on-dock rail at the primary terminal and the smaller inland network vs. Savannah and Virginia constrain the score.

Dimension 6 — Governance & Strategic Positioning

Single-Purpose State Authority — Agile Governance With Capital Risk

The SC Ports Authority is a state authority of South Carolina, created by the General Assembly and governed by a Board of Directors appointed by the Governor. It operates as an enterprise fund, nominally self-supporting through revenue bond financing and operating revenues — with the state providing grants and appropriations as a supplemental backstop. This is a clean, single-purpose governance structure that allows SC Ports to make port-specific capital decisions without competing against airport, highway, or rail priorities at the board level.

The HLT full build-out is the defining strategic commitment. Its planned 2.4 million TEU capacity adds sufficient runway to grow Charleston from a 2.5 million TEU port to a potential 4.5–5.0 million TEU port over a 10–15 year horizon — a positioning that would vault Charleston past Virginia and challenge Savannah's East Coast #2 status behind NY/NJ.

Primary Governance Risk

Capital concentration: with $1.4B in long-term debt almost entirely attributable to HLT, financial health is heavily dependent on HLT volume ramp-up proceeding on schedule.

Score: 78/100

Single-purpose state authority governance is a strength. HLT vision is clearly defined and ambitious. Deductions reflect capital concentration risk, state appropriations dependence, and the strategic vision still being mid-execution.

Strategic Opportunity Areas

The Path to Closing the 15-Point Gap to #1

  • Priority 1 — Accelerate HLT Phase 2+: Every year of delay is a year of capacity constraint forcing carriers to redirect volume to Savannah or Virginia. HLT full build-out transforms Charleston into a 4.0–4.5M TEU competitive platform.
  • Priority 2 — On-Dock Rail at HLT: HLT on a former Navy Base site with substantial surrounding land is the most feasible location for on-dock rail in the SC Ports system. CSX or NS on-dock service at HLT directly addresses the 20-point connectivity gap vs. Savannah.
  • Priority 3 — Third Inland Port (Charlotte/Western NC): Charlotte is the largest major Southeast freight market not firmly served by Charleston's existing inland ports. A third facility rail-connected to HLT would extend effective hinterland into the country's fastest-growing major metro area.
  • Priority 4 — Revenue Diversification: Container storage optimization (premium dwell pricing), cruise expansion (second home-port berth), HLT logistics park development (former Navy Base land), and federal PIDP/INFRA grant capture to reduce state dependency.
  • Priority 5 — Automation Evaluation at HLT Phase 2: A formal ASC feasibility assessment timed to Phase 2 build-out, when new cranes will be procured in any case, would deliver measurable CPPI improvement and signal to premium ULCV services that Charleston is committed to world-class terminal operations.
