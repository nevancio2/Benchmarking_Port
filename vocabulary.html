Benchmarking Glossary

Key terms and definitions used consistently across the NCDOT Global Transit Benchmarking Research. Understanding these concepts is essential for interpreting scores, comparisons, and recommendations in the context of North Carolina's statewide transit landscape.

Finance & Revenue Terms
01 Fare Recovery Ratio Finance

The percentage of a transit agency's total operating costs that are covered by passenger fare revenue. A fare recovery ratio of 60% means fares pay for 60 cents of every dollar spent on operations, with the remaining 40% funded by subsidies, grants, or non-fare revenue.

Why it matters: Several systems in this benchmark are compared on fare recovery, and some readers may confuse this with total cost recovery (which includes subsidies and non-fare revenue). A high fare recovery ratio does not mean a system is independent of government support — capital costs may be publicly funded separately (see: Singapore NRFF, SBB infrastructure subsidies).

Singapore ~55% (operating-cost basis; ~20% govt capital excluded)Tokyo ~58%MTA ~35%NCDOT (NC avg) ~20–25% [TO BE VERIFIED via UPTAS]
02 Non-Fare Revenue Finance

Revenue not directly collected from passenger fares. Includes advertising, retail concessions, toll revenue, data licensing, property development, payment processing fees, parking, naming rights, and international rail pass sales.

The strategic importance of non-fare revenue is a central theme of this benchmark. Systems with high non-fare revenue are less vulnerable to ridership fluctuations and political pressure on fare levels.

TfL: Congestion Charge, ULEZJR East: Suica fees, retailSBB: Freight, real estate
03 Subsidy / Government Support Finance

Public funding provided through state, local, regional, or federal sources to support capital or operating costs of a transit agency. Two types are distinct: operating subsidy (covers day-to-day operations) and capital support (funds infrastructure, vehicles, and major projects).

Precision matters here: Singapore LTA is often cited at ~70% fare recovery, but this figure covers only operating costs under a model where the government already owns all rail assets (NRFF) and absorbs all bus revenue risk (BCM). When full government capital support is counted, operating fare recovery is closer to ~55%. This distinction is important: a high operating fare recovery does not mean a system is independent of government support.

NCDOT/NC operators: ~70–75% of ops from state/federal subsidy [TO BE VERIFIED]Singapore NRFF: govt owns rail assetsSBB: federal infrastructure grants
04 Revenue Diversification Finance

The extent to which a transit agency relies on multiple income streams rather than fares and direct government subsidy alone. A diversified revenue base reduces financial risk and supports investment in service quality independent of political budget cycles.

NCDOT and NC transit operators score lowest in this benchmark on revenue diversification — they have virtually none of the coordinated non-fare revenue streams that sustain peer systems. Revenue diversification, coordinated through NCDOT PTD, is identified as the highest-priority strategic opportunity for North Carolina.

05 Payment Processing Fees Finance

Revenue earned when a transit-linked payment instrument is used for non-transit purchases and the operator or ecosystem captures a small fee per transaction. Aggregated across millions of daily purchases, these fees generate significant revenue independent of ridership.

Tokyo's Suica card earns processing fees on every retail purchase at 500,000+ acceptance points. Seoul Metro earns T-money processing fees at 100,000+ retail locations. GoTriangle's GoPass/UMO pilot in the Triangle is NC's seed of this model — scaling this statewide across all NCDOT-supported operators is the direct NC application.

Suica (Tokyo)T-money (Seoul)EZ-Link (Singapore)
06 Joint Development / Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) Finance

Real estate development on, above, or adjacent to transit property that generates revenue through leases, land sales, air rights, or increased ridership from new transit-adjacent residents and workers. TOD also increases surrounding property tax revenue for local governments, though transit agencies may capture only a portion of this value directly.

WMATA: 20+ TOD projectsJR East: Lumine retail above stationsSBB: 800+ station commercial spaces
Payment System Terms
07 Open-Loop Contactless Payment Payment

Payment using a standard bank card or mobile wallet (Visa, Mastercard, Apple Pay, Google Pay) directly at the fare gate or validator, without requiring a transit-issued proprietary card. No registration, no pre-loading, no local card needed.

TfL pioneered open-loop contactless on London buses in December 2012, extending to the full network in September 2014. MTA's OMNY program is the largest open-loop transit rollout in US history. North Carolina has no statewide contactless standard — GoTriangle's GoPass/UMO pilot is the most advanced NC deployment, with statewide coordination through NCDOT PTD as the recommended next step.

London OMNYMTA OMNYSingapore SimplyGo
08 Closed-Loop Payment Payment

Payment using a transit-issued proprietary card or digital wallet that is pre-loaded with value and only accepted within the transit ecosystem and designated partner merchants. Requires onboarding by the rider before first use.

Closed-loop systems can evolve into powerful payment platforms if retail acceptance is expanded (e.g., Suica, T-money, EZ-Link). The friction of obtaining a closed-loop card creates onboarding barriers for tourists and infrequent users.

SmarTrip (WMATA)TAP (LA Metro)EZ-Link (Singapore)T-money (Seoul)Suica (Tokyo)
09 EMV Payment

Europay, Mastercard, Visa — the global technical standard for chip-enabled bank cards and secure contactless transactions. Commonly used as shorthand for bank-card tap payment in transit. EMV contactless allows riders to tap standard bank cards at fare validators.

Used by TfL, MTA OMNY, Singapore SimplyGo
10 Fare Capping Payment

A feature of contactless payment systems that automatically limits the total amount a rider pays within a defined period (daily or weekly), regardless of the number of trips taken. Once the cap is reached, additional trips within the period are free. Prevents riders from overpaying versus a period pass.

MTA's OMNY includes a weekly fare cap equivalent to the 7-day unlimited MetroCard ($34 as of the benchmarked period). Daily and weekly caps should be stated separately — a common error is listing both as the same figure.

MTA OMNY weekly cap: $34TfL daily and weekly caps on Oyster/contactless
Technology Terms
11 GTFS-RT Technology

General Transit Feed Specification – Realtime. A standard format for publishing live transit information, including vehicle positions, arrival predictions, and service alerts. GTFS-RT is the foundation for real-time transit data in third-party apps like Google Maps, Transit App, and Citymapper.

North Carolina lacks a statewide GTFS-RT aggregation portal. Individual NC operators have varying levels of real-time data publication, but no unified open API exists. Every top-5 system in this benchmark publishes comprehensive GTFS-RT. A statewide NCDOT open data mandate for all state-funded operators is identified as the highest-priority near-term technology action.

Used by MTA, TfL, Singapore LTA, Seoul MetroStatewide coordination absent: NCDOT
12 API / Open Data Technology

An Application Programming Interface (API) is a protocol that allows third-party developers and systems to access transit data. "Open data" means the agency makes such information broadly available — rather than restricting it to internal tools — enabling a developer ecosystem to build rider-facing applications at no cost to the agency.

TfL's 2010 open data decision catalyzed 700+ third-party apps and generated an estimated £130M in annual economic value for London (2017 Deloitte study). Singapore's LTA DataMall is among the first comprehensive multimodal open transit data platforms globally.

13 Data Licensing Data

The monetization of anonymized or aggregated mobility data through paid access, commercial licensing, or structured data products. Customers include urban planners, real estate developers, logistics companies, retailers, and researchers seeking insights from large-scale movement data.

Singapore LTA's DataMall is an example of an open data platform that has evolved toward commercial licensing. North Carolina's transit operators collectively generate millions of anonymized mobility data points daily and currently earn $0 from coordinated data licensing — one of the most significant missed revenue opportunities identified in this benchmark.

Singapore LTA DataMallSeoul Metro Smart City partnershipsNCDOT statewide: $0 currently
14 Real-Time Crowding Data Technology

Live information on passenger density by vehicle, car, platform, or station used to help riders choose less crowded options before and during their journey. Typically generated by weight sensors in train cars, gate tap counts, or camera-based AI systems.

Seoul Metro's car-level crowding displays — showing real-time passenger density per individual train car before the train arrives — are a documented world first. WMATA and MTA both have passenger counting infrastructure but do not currently publish real-time crowding data to riders.

Seoul Metro: per-car display on platformsTfL: crowding levels in APIWMATA: data exists but not published
Customer Experience Terms
15 Silo Integration UX

The degree to which pre-trip, in-trip, and post-trip services are unified across operators, apps, fares, and data systems. A fully integrated system allows a rider to plan, pay, board, transfer, and review a multi-operator journey without switching apps, re-entering information, or losing data continuity between phases.

This benchmark breaks silo integration into three phases: Pre-Transit (planning, booking, first-mile), During Transit (live updates, crowding, alerts), and Post-Transit (history, loyalty, recommendations). Singapore and Zürich score highest on silo integration; NCDOT scores lowest due to structural fragmentation across dozens of independent operators with no statewide unified platform.

16 Pre-Transit / During Transit / Post-Transit UX

The three phases of the customer journey used to evaluate data connectivity in this benchmark:

Pre-Transit: Planning, booking, ticket purchase, parking, and first/last-mile arrangements before travel begins.

During Transit: Live status, real-time crowding, delays, alerts, connections, and onboard or in-station information during the trip.

Post-Transit: Trip history, receipts, loyalty, recommendations, feedback, and service recovery after the trip ends.

No US system in this benchmark achieves "Fully Connected" on all three phases. Singapore and Zürich do.

17 First/Last Mile Integration UX

Integration of transit with rideshare, bikes, scooters, parking, and walking connections needed to complete the trip from the rider's origin to the transit station, and from the destination station to the rider's final destination. The "first mile" and "last mile" are often the most friction-laden parts of a transit journey.

Singapore: bike-share booking in MyTransport appSBB: car-share in SBB MobileLA Metro: first/last mile gap is primary UX challenge
18 Unified Digital Front Door UX

A single app or digital interface that lets riders plan, pay, travel, and review their complete journey without switching between systems or tools. The "front door" metaphor captures the idea that riders should have one entry point to the entire transit ecosystem, regardless of how many operators, modes, or agencies are involved in their trip.

Singapore: MyTransport.SGZürich: SBB MobileNCDOT: separate apps per operator; GoTriangle GoPass partially integrates Triangle operators
19 Personalization Layer UX

Use of rider journey data to tailor suggestions, alerts, loyalty offers, disruption alternatives, and post-journey recommendations to the individual rider. A personalization layer turns anonymized mobility data into a rider-facing benefit — proactive journey suggestions, relevant alerts, and rewards aligned with actual travel patterns.

The absence of a personalization layer is a common gap across US systems in this benchmark. OMNY, SmarTrip, and TAP all generate rich journey data that is not currently surfaced to riders in personalized form.

20 Customer Centricity UX

The degree to which a transit system is designed around the rider's complete journey experience, rather than the operational or administrative boundaries of individual agencies and operators. A customer-centric system prioritizes the rider's perspective across all touchpoints: planning, payment, travel, and follow-up.

Singapore and Zürich score highest on customer centricity — their systems are designed so that operational complexity is hidden behind a simple rider experience. NCDOT's "multi-operator, multi-app problem" is the clearest benchmark expression of low customer centricity: structural fragmentation across dozens of independent NC operators is directly visible and painful to riders crossing operator boundaries. GoTriangle's GoPass pilot is the most significant step toward rider-centric design in NC.

Benchmark Methodology
21 Benchmark Score Methodology

A comparative score assigned within this research framework across four dimensions: Technology Stack, User Experience, Silo Integration, and Revenue Model. Scores are on a 0–100 scale and reflect the research team's assessment based on publicly available information, agency reports, and direct product evaluation.

Important: These scores are internal to the NCDOT Benchmarking Research 2024 study. They are not independently validated external industry ratings, and should not be cited as such. The methodology weights each dimension equally; no external tie-breaking methodology is applied. NCDOT figures represent the statewide coordination picture and are estimated pending verification from NCDOT UPTAS reports and individual operator financials.

22 New Rail Financing Framework (NRFF) Governance

Singapore's government framework, introduced in 2010, under which the Land Transport Authority (LTA) owns all rail operating assets — trains, tracks, and signaling equipment — and operators pay a license fee to run services. This means the government bears all capital risk, and operators focus purely on service delivery.

The NRFF fundamentally changes how to interpret Singapore's "high fare recovery": fares cover operating costs in a model where the government has already absorbed the capital investment. Singapore's financial model is not as subsidy-independent as a standalone fare recovery figure suggests.

23 Bus Contracting Model (BCM) Governance

Singapore's bus financing model, introduced in 2016, under which the government (through LTA) bears all revenue risk — setting fares and absorbing fare volatility — while bus operators are paid a fee to operate services to quality standards. This model insulates operators from ridership risk and gives LTA full control over network planning.

Together with the NRFF, the BCM means Singapore's government is deeply involved in financing public transit, even though the systems present high fare recovery ratios. This context is essential for fair cross-system comparison.
