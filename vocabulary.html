Port Operations Terms
01 TEU (Twenty-Foot Equivalent Unit) Operations

The standard unit of container measurement in port logistics. A TEU is equivalent to one 20-foot intermodal shipping container. A 40-foot container counts as 2 TEUs. Annual TEU throughput is the foundational metric for comparing port size, market share, and revenue capacity.

Why it matters: Volume alone does not determine the benchmark score — growth trajectory and utilization rate relative to capacity also factor in. A port growing 9% annually scores higher than a larger port growing under 1% in a comparable period. All six ports in this benchmark are measured in annual TEUs as their primary throughput metric.

NY/NJ: ~9.0M TEUs (2024)Savannah: ~5.9M TEUs (CY2025)Virginia: ~3.5M TEUs (FY2024)Charleston: ~2.5M TEUs (FY2025)
02 CPPI — Container Port Performance Index Operations

The World Bank Container Port Performance Index (CPPI), published annually by the World Bank and S&P Global Market Intelligence, measures how efficiently vessels are served at each port. The primary metric is MPPH — Moves Per Port Hour — the number of container lifts performed per total hour of port time from vessel arrival through departure. Higher MPPH means faster vessel turnaround, lower cost per lift for carriers, and greater port attractiveness for premium vessel services.

U.S. East Coast ports as a class have historically ranked in the lower half of global CPPI scores, due to ILA labor agreements constraining automation, multi-terminal fragmentation, and channel depth limits. Port Everglades achieved top 20% worldwide CPPI ranking (confirmed September 2025). Virginia is the consistent top CPPI performer on the East Coast. The CPPI is the anchor data source for Dimension 2 (Operational Efficiency) in this benchmark.

Virginia: East Coast CPPI leaderPort Everglades: Top 20% worldwide (confirmed)NY/NJ: Mid-range (multi-terminal drag)
03 Moves Per Port Hour (MPPH) Operations

The number of container lifts (moves) completed per total hour of port time, calculated from vessel arrival at anchorage through departure. MPPH is the CPPI's primary efficiency metric. It captures not just how fast cranes work, but how much time is spent waiting (at anchor, waiting for berth, waiting for crane deployment) vs. actively working cargo.

A port where a vessel sits at anchor for 12 hours before berthing will have a lower MPPH than a port where the same vessel berths immediately upon arrival — even if crane productivity (gross crane rate) is identical. This is why channel depth and berth availability are CPPI contributors, not just crane counts.

Higher is betterAutomation (ASCs) is the strongest MPPH driverDeep channels reduce anchor waiting time
04 ASC — Automated Stacking Crane Operations

A computer-controlled crane system that moves and stacks containers in a terminal yard without human operators. ASCs replace rubber-tired gantry cranes (RTGs) operated by human drivers. They reduce cycle time, labor cost per lift, and operational variability — the primary drivers of higher CPPI scores. ASC deployment consistently correlates with top CPPI rankings in the World Bank's multi-year analysis.

Virginia International Gateway (VIG) is the only terminal in this benchmark operating ASCs at scale — the primary reason Virginia is the East Coast's CPPI leader. ILA (International Longshoremen's Association) labor agreements have historically constrained automation deployment at U.S. East Coast ports. The 2025 ILA–USMX contract negotiations directly affected the viability of further automation expansion at all six ports in this benchmark.

Virginia VIG: ASCs deployedCharleston HLT: ASC evaluation recommendedSavannah GCT: RTGs (no ASCs)
05 Container Dwell Time Operations

The average number of days a container remains in the terminal after being discharged from a vessel, before being picked up by truck or train. Lower dwell time means faster terminal yard turnover, less congestion, and higher effective capacity — a port with 2-day average dwell time can theoretically handle more TEUs through the same physical space than a port with 5-day average dwell time. Dwell time is also a revenue variable: many ports charge escalating storage fees after a free-time period, making excessive dwell a secondary revenue source.

Savannah's Mason Mega Rail Terminal directly reduces container dwell time by enabling direct vessel-to-train transfers, eliminating the intermediate truck dray and yard staging steps. This is why on-dock rail is a CPPI contributor, not just a connectivity asset.

On-dock rail reduces dwell timeStorage fees charged after free-time periodHigh dwell = reduced effective capacity
06 STS Crane (Ship-to-Shore Crane) Operations

A large gantry crane mounted on rails at the waterfront, used to load and unload containers between vessel and terminal. STS crane count is a proxy for simultaneous vessel-handling capacity — more cranes mean more berths can be actively worked or more cranes can be deployed per vessel call. STS cranes are classified by the size of vessel they can serve: post-Panamax cranes serve vessels up to approximately 18,000 TEU; super post-Panamax cranes serve the largest ULCVs of 18,000–24,000+ TEU.

Savannah: 38 STS cranes (largest in benchmark)Virginia: 29 STS cranesCharleston: ~18 STS cranes (14 WWT + 4 HLT P1)Port Everglades/Miami: ~6–8 each
Infrastructure Terms
07 Channel Depth (Draft) Infrastructure

The depth of water in the approach channel and harbor, measured in feet at Mean Low Water (MLW). Channel depth is the single most consequential physical variable determining which vessels can call a port fully loaded. A fully loaded ULCV of 20,000 TEU draws approximately 49–50 feet of water. A port with a 47-foot channel (Savannah) must receive ULCVs partially empty; a port with a 52-foot channel (Charleston) or 55-foot channel (Virginia) receives fully loaded ULCVs at any tide.

Why it matters for carriers: A carrier routing a 20,000-TEU vessel to a 47-foot port cannot fill it to capacity. Every container left behind is revenue lost, which raises effective per-TEU cost. Carriers optimize vessel assignments toward ports where they can maximize load — giving deeper-channel ports a structural competitive advantage in attracting the largest, most cost-efficient vessel classes.

Virginia: 55 ft (deepest East Coast, near completion)Charleston: 52 ftNY/NJ, Everglades, Miami: 50 ftSavannah: 47 ft (shallowest in benchmark)
08 ULCV — Ultra Large Container Vessel Infrastructure

A container vessel of 18,000–24,000+ TEU capacity. ULCVs represent the newest generation of container ships and are being rapidly deployed on major trade lanes (Asia–Europe, Asia–East Coast via Suez) because their scale reduces per-TEU fuel and operating costs. Their deeper drafts (49–51 feet fully loaded) and wider beams require both channel depth and sufficient crane outreach (super post-Panamax cranes) to serve them productively.

The proliferation of ULCVs is the defining infrastructure challenge for all six ports in this benchmark. Virginia's 55-foot channel is the only East Coast channel that can receive all currently operating ULCVs fully loaded. Charleston's 52-foot channel can receive most ULCVs. Savannah's 47-foot channel cannot receive the largest ULCVs fully loaded, which is the primary driver of GPA's June 2026 channel deepening feasibility study.

Typical ULCV draft: 49–51 ft fully loadedVirginia 55 ft: can receive all ULCVsSavannah 47 ft: draft restrictions on largest ULCVs
09 HLT — Hugh K. Leatherman Terminal Infrastructure

SC Ports' new container terminal under construction on the former Navy Base site on the Cooper River in North Charleston. Phase 1 is operational with approximately 1,400 linear feet of berth, 4 super post-Panamax STS cranes, and approximately 700,000 TEUs of annual capacity. Full build-out is planned at approximately 2.4 million TEUs annually — which, when complete, would push SC Ports' total container capacity from approximately 2.5 million TEUs to approximately 4.0–4.5 million TEUs.

HLT is the single most important strategic asset in the SC Ports Authority portfolio. It is the primary near-term growth catalyst for Charleston's Throughput, Infrastructure, Connectivity, and Financial dimension scores. Accelerating HLT later phases is the highest-priority strategic action identified in this benchmark's gap analysis.

Phase 1: ~700K TEU capacity (operational)Full build-out: ~2.4M TEUCapital program: $1.4B (SC Ports revenue bonds)
10 Bayonne Bridge (Air Draft Clearance) Infrastructure

The Bayonne Bridge spans the Kill Van Kull waterway between Bayonne, NJ and Staten Island, NY — the primary maritime access route to Port Newark Container Terminal and the main Newark Bay terminals of NY/NJ. The bridge's original height of 151 feet physically blocked neo-Panamax and larger vessels from reaching these terminals. The $1.7 billion bridge-raising project, completed in 2019, elevated the air draft clearance to 215 feet — unlocking larger vessel calls to the core NY/NJ terminals.

The Bayonne Bridge raising is one of the most consequential East Coast port infrastructure investments of the last decade. It is the reason NY/NJ can serve as a top-tier gateway for modern large container vessels. Without it, APM Terminals Bayonne and GCT New York (Howland Hook) would have been physically unable to serve the vessels that now call regularly.

Original height: 151 ft (blocked neo-Panamax)Current height: 215 ft (completed 2019)Cost: ~$1.7 billion
11 Gateway Investment Program (Virginia) Infrastructure

The Port of Virginia's $1.4 billion capital improvement program encompassing: channel dredging to 55 feet (nearing completion January 2026); channel widening to enable simultaneous dual-ULCV transit; a new ULCV berth at Norfolk International Terminals; Automated Stacking Crane (ASC) deployment at Virginia International Gateway; and $83 million in on-dock rail infrastructure upgrades. The program is substantially complete as of early 2026.

The Gateway Investment Program is the benchmark comparison for SC Ports' HLT program. Virginia executed a $1.4 billion capital program on a stronger operating income foundation ($113M vs. Charleston's $26.7M), funded through a diversified mix of revenue bonds, federal PIDP and INFRA grants, and state contributions — a capital funding model that SC Ports should benchmark for future HLT phases.

Total: $1.4 billion55-ft channel, ASCs, on-dock rail, new ULCV berthSubstantially complete as of Q1 2026
Logistics & Intermodal Terms
12 On-Dock Rail Logistics

Rail tracks installed directly within the container terminal, enabling containers to be moved from vessel to railcar (or vice versa) without an intermediate truck dray to an off-dock rail ramp. On-dock rail is the gold standard of port intermodal connectivity: it eliminates the dray, reduces container dwell time, lowers total supply chain cost per TEU, and extends the port's effective hinterland reach deep into the national rail network.

Virginia has on-dock rail at both VIG and NIT. Savannah's Mason Mega Rail Terminal is North America's largest on-dock rail facility. Charleston lacks on-dock rail at Wando Welch Terminal (containers must be truck-drayed to an off-terminal rail ramp). HLT represents the best opportunity to add on-dock rail to the SC Ports system — it is the single highest-connectivity leverage point in the benchmark gap analysis for Charleston.

Virginia VIG + NIT: on-dock rail (gold standard)Savannah Mason Mega Rail: North America's largestCharleston WWT: no on-dock rail (gap)Port Everglades, PortMiami: no on-dock rail
13 Mason Mega Rail Terminal (Savannah) Logistics

The on-dock rail facility at Garden City Terminal, Savannah — North America's largest on-dock rail terminal by capacity and throughput. The Mason Terminal enables direct vessel-to-train container transfers, serving both CSX and Norfolk Southern. GPA's target is a 40%+ rail modal share for container movements, which would dramatically reduce truck dependency and extend effective hinterland reach into the Midwest and beyond. Appalachian Regional Port (ARP) in Chatsworth, GA, set a new record of 45,700 containers in CY2025 (+19% YoY), demonstrating GPA's expanding inland rail reach.

North America's largest on-dock rail facilityServes CSX and Norfolk Southern directly40% rail modal share target
14 Inland Port Logistics

A facility located inland — away from the marine terminal — that handles container transfers between trucks and trains, enabling shippers in interior markets to access a seaport's services without trucking containers all the way to the coast. A rail-connected inland port effectively extends a port's geographic hinterland to include cities and industrial corridors hundreds of miles from the waterfront.

Inland port networks directly determine a port's ability to compete for cargo generated beyond the immediate coastal metro area. Savannah has two inland ports (ARP Chatsworth; Blue Ridge Gainesville); Virginia has one (Front Royal/Shenandoah); SC Ports has two (Greer, Dillon). Charleston's strategic gap analysis identifies a third inland port in the Charlotte, NC corridor as the highest-priority connectivity investment.

SC Ports: Inland Port Greer (NS, Upstate SC) + Inland Port Dillon (CSX, I-95 corridor)GPA: ARP Chatsworth + Blue Ridge Gainesville (Atlanta market)Virginia: Front Royal VIP (Shenandoah Valley / DC-west market)
15 Modal Split (Rail vs. Truck) Logistics

The percentage of container movements handled by rail vs. truck. A higher rail modal share generally means lower per-TEU logistics cost, lower emissions, greater geographic reach, and less sensitivity to regional truck driver capacity constraints. A truck-dominant modal split (like Port Everglades and PortMiami) limits hinterland reach to markets reachable by truck within economical distance from the port.

Savannah target: 40%+ rail shareVirginia: strong rail share (on-dock at VIG + NIT)Port Everglades: truck-only (no rail program)PortMiami: truck-only (no rail program)
16 ExpressRail (NY/NJ) Logistics

PANYNJ's on-dock rail program at Port Newark Container Terminal and Howland Hook Marine Terminal. ExpressRail is a PANYNJ-owned rail infrastructure investment that provides on-dock rail access to the NY/NJ port complex — superior to Port Everglades and PortMiami (no rail programs) but smaller in scale and strategic depth than Virginia's dual-terminal on-dock rail and Savannah's Mason Mega Rail Terminal. Rail intermodal modal share at NY/NJ is lower than Virginia and Savannah relative to total TEUs, reflecting the Northeast's truck-dominant logistics model.

PANYNJ-owned on-dock rail at PNCT + Howland HookLess comprehensive than Virginia or Savannah programs
Finance & Revenue Terms
17 Revenue Per TEU Finance

Total operating revenues divided by total annual TEU throughput. A normalized efficiency metric for cross-port comparison that captures how much operating revenue a port generates per container handled, regardless of absolute size. Revenue per TEU reflects both the value-add of the port's services and its governance model: direct-operator ports (Savannah, Virginia) capture more per-TEU revenue than landlord ports (PANYNJ) that share terminal revenue with private operators.

Revenue per TEU reveals a counterintuitive pattern: the largest ports by volume have the lowest revenue per TEU (PANYNJ at ~$50–56/TEU is the most extreme case of landlord model dilution on massive volume), while mid-size ports with diversified revenue streams have the highest (Port Everglades at ~$187–196/TEU from petroleum + cruise + containers).

Port Everglades: ~$187–196/TEUCharleston: ~$169/TEUVirginia: ~$128–157/TEUSavannah: ~$127/TEUPANYNJ (port segment): ~$50–56/TEU
18 Operator Model vs. Landlord Model Finance

Two distinct port governance and revenue structures:

Operator Model: The port authority directly operates the terminal, retaining 100% of wharfage, container handling charges, storage fees, equipment rental, and dockage. No revenue is shared with a private terminal operator. Examples: Georgia Ports Authority (Garden City Terminal), Port of Virginia (via VIT subsidiary).

Landlord Model: The port authority owns the land and infrastructure but leases terminal operations to private companies (APM Terminals, GCT, PNCT, etc.). The authority receives lease fees rather than the full terminal operating revenue. Example: PANYNJ (leases to APM Terminals, GCT Bayonne, GCT New York, PNCT, Red Hook).

This is the single most important revenue model insight in the benchmark. The landlord vs. operator gap translates to approximately a 2.5x revenue-per-TEU differential: Savannah (operator) generates ~$127/TEU while PANYNJ (landlord) generates ~$50–56/TEU on port revenues alone.

Operator: Savannah ($698.7M on 5.5M TEUs)Landlord: PANYNJ (~$450–500M on 9.0M TEUs port-only)
19 Revenue Diversification (Tri-Sector Model) Finance

The extent to which a port's revenues come from multiple, non-correlated sectors rather than container handling alone. Port Everglades is the benchmark leader in revenue diversification: its three-pillar model — containers (~60–65%), petroleum (~15–20%), and cruise (~20–25%) — provides resilience when any single sector weakens. A shipping market downturn that reduces container revenue does not reduce petroleum distribution fees or cruise homeport charges.

The parallel in port finance to the transit benchmark's finding: "systems with high non-fare revenue are less vulnerable to ridership fluctuations." Port Everglades' FY2024 operating margin of 29.7% is the highest confirmed in the benchmark — during the same post-pandemic freight rate normalization that produced Charleston's FY2024 operating loss of ($2.3M). The single-revenue-stream vulnerability is directly demonstrated by this comparison.

Port Everglades: containers + petroleum + cruisePANYNJ: airports + bridges/tunnels + port + PATHCharleston: containers dominant (~50% of total revenues)
20 Operating Income Margin Finance

Operating income (revenues minus operating expenses, before interest and non-operating items) divided by total operating revenues, expressed as a percentage. Operating margin indicates how much of each revenue dollar is retained as operating profit. For capital-intensive infrastructure businesses like ports, a thin operating margin is a financial risk signal because debt service payments (interest and principal on revenue bonds) must be paid from operating income supplemented by non-operating revenues.

Port Everglades FY2024: 29.7% ($64.0M on $215.5M)Virginia FY2024: ~20–25% ($113M operating income, estimated revenues)Charleston FY2025: 6.3% ($26.7M on $426.5M)Charleston FY2024: negative ($2.3M operating loss)
21 Revenue Bonds / Bond Rating Finance

Revenue bonds are municipal debt instruments repaid from a specific revenue stream — in port finance, typically port operating revenues (wharfage, handling fees, storage, etc.). They are the primary capital financing instrument for independent port authorities. Bond ratings assigned by Fitch, S&P, and Moody's reflect the creditworthiness of the bonds and directly determine borrowing cost. Higher ratings mean lower interest rates on capital projects.

PANYNJ holds the highest confirmed rating in this benchmark (Fitch AA-) driven by multi-facility revenue diversification. GPA holds an S&P "excellent" (AA-range) reaffirmed August 2025 during active expansion. PortMiami holds the lowest confirmed rating (Fitch 'A' stable), reflecting the cruise revenue concentration and Deep Dredge debt service burden. SC Ports holds an investment-grade rating with $1.4B in outstanding revenue bonds as of June 30, 2025.

PANYNJ: AA- (Fitch) — highest in benchmarkGPA: S&P "Excellent" (AA-range)PortMiami: A (Fitch) — lowest confirmed

Governance & Methodology
27 Port Authority (State Authority) Governance

A public entity created by state legislation with a dedicated mandate to own, develop, and operate port infrastructure. State port authorities (SC Ports, Virginia Port Authority, Georgia Ports Authority) are independent of general state budget processes and can issue tax-exempt revenue bonds, set rates, and make capital decisions through their own boards — without competing against highway, school, or public safety priorities in a general appropriations process.

State authority governance is the structural advantage that enables SC Ports, Virginia, and GPA to execute billion-dollar capital programs with relative speed and decisiveness. The $1.4 billion HLT bond program (SC Ports) and the $1.4 billion Gateway Investment Program (Virginia) were funded through independent revenue bond authority — a financing speed and flexibility that county-operated Port Everglades and PortMiami do not possess.

28 County-Operated Port (Broward County / Miami-Dade) Governance

Port Everglades (Broward County) and PortMiami (Miami-Dade County) are operated as departments of their respective county governments — not as independent port authorities. This means port capital investment competes with roads, schools, public safety, and every other county service at the county commission level. Port bond issuances require county approval, and major strategic investments go through multi-year county planning and budget processes rather than independent board votes.

The governance constraint is the primary reason Port Everglades' 2024 Master/Vision Plan expansion is slower to execute than comparable programs at SC Ports or GPA. The county model provides implicit financial backstop (county credit supports bonds) but reduces decision-making speed — the direct parallel to the governance fragmentation problem identified in the transit benchmark.

Port Everglades: Broward County dept.PortMiami: Miami-Dade County enterprise dept.Advantage: county credit backstopDisadvantage: capital competes with other county priorities
29 PANYNJ — Bi-State Compact Authority Governance

The Port Authority of New York and New Jersey is a bi-state compact agency created in 1921 by agreement between the states of New York and New Jersey. It is governed by commissioners appointed by two governors with independent and sometimes competing political agendas. The Port Authority operates airports (JFK, LaGuardia, Newark Liberty), bridges and tunnels (George Washington Bridge, Holland Tunnel, Lincoln Tunnel, Goethals Bridge), port marine terminals, and PATH rail under a single agency umbrella.

PANYNJ's multi-facility diversification is simultaneously its greatest financial strength (AA- Fitch rating from diversified multi-sector revenues) and its governance constraint for port-specific decision-making (port capital competes with airport redevelopments, bridge/tunnel programs, and PATH modernization for board attention and capital allocation). PANYNJ's port division cannot independently set port capital priorities without bi-state board approval — the exact governance fragmentation problem identified in this benchmark as a root cause of NY/NJ's lower governance score.

30 Benchmark Score — Methodology Note Methodology

All benchmark scores in this study are on a 0–100 scale across six equally weighted dimensions (1/6 each): Throughput & Scale, Operational Efficiency (CPPI), Infrastructure & Physical Capacity, Financial Performance & Revenue Model, Connectivity & Hinterland Reach, and Governance & Strategic Positioning. The six dimension scores are averaged into an overall score. Where two ports tie overall (Savannah and Virginia at 89/100), the tiebreaker is the Throughput & Scale dimension score.

Important: All scores are internal to the U.S. East Coast Container Port Benchmarking Study 2025. They are not independently validated external industry ratings, and should not be cited as such. They reflect the research team's assessment based on publicly available audited financial statements, World Bank CPPI data, BTS Port Performance statistics, Fitch/S&P rating analyses, and official port press releases. All financial figures are from the most recently completed audited fiscal year for each port authority as of the research date.
